## Critical-Thinking Methods (v2.1)

### Method: Falsification (Karl Popper)

**What it is:** Rather than trying to confirm a claim, you actively attempt to falsify it. A claim that survives a genuine falsification attempt is stronger than one that merely accumulates confirming sources.



**Why it is in this prompt:** The Kohärenz-Protokoll framework makes bold theoretical claims grounded in hard physics and mathematics. These claims are falsifiable in principle — they can be wrong. Applying falsification thinking reveals whether the framework's claims are robust or dependent on borderline interpretations.



**How to apply it — step by step:**



1.  For each major theoretical claim in the seven axes, state its negation explicitly. Example: Claim = "Landauer's Principle applies to narrative erasure." Negation = "Landauer's Principle does not model narrative information loss."



1.  Search for evidence that would **support the negation.** Look for papers that challenge Landauer's applicability to information erasure, or that propose alternative models. Do not search for confirmations — search for challenges.



1.  If you find strong falsifying evidence, record it. If you find only weak challenges or none at all, note that the claim has survived falsification attempts.



1.  Update your confidence: claims that survive genuine falsification attempts deserve higher weight than claims that merely accumulate confirmations.



**When to stop / escape criterion:** Stop when you have found either (a) a peer-reviewed paper that directly challenges the claim, or (b) three independent searches for falsifying evidence all return no results. The absence of falsification does not prove truth, but it indicates robustness.



**Example trigger in this research context:** For "DKT treats trauma as a K\_1 structure buffering against K\_0 erasure," search for psychological literature that proposes trauma serves a different function or that the K\_1/K\_0 distinction is not valid for trauma modeling.



-----

### Method: Source Triangulation

**What it is:** Every significant factual claim is confirmed across at least **three independent source types** before being admitted to the final output. Aggregators count as one source, not three.



**Why it is in this prompt:** Single-source claims propagate errors. Aggregator-heavy research is especially prone to citation chains that trace back to one primary source that is wrong. The Kohärenz-Protokoll framework makes claims spanning physics, mathematics, and psychology — each domain has its own authoritative sources, and your job is to cross-verify.



**How to apply it — step by step:**



1.  For every significant claim, identify the **primary source** (the original research, paper, dataset, or firsthand report).



1.  Find at least **two additional independent sources** of different type. Example: if the primary source is a physics paper, find confirmation from (a) a separate physics paper, (b) a mathematics textbook on Leech Lattices, (c) a monograph on Monster Group theory.



1.  If all confirmations trace back to a single primary source, mark the claim as **single-source** and flag it in the Contradiction Log.



1.  Prefer source types in this order:



  - (a) Peer-reviewed papers (primary research)
  - (b) Monographs and textbooks by established scholars
  - (c) Official databases (arXiv, JSTOR, PubMed)
  - (d) Conference proceedings and research institutes
  - (e) News summaries of research (only for context)



**When to stop / escape criterion:** Stop once three independent, non-aggregated confirmations are found, OR when the claim is sufficiently minor that single-source citation is acceptable (then flag it). Do not over-search diminishing returns.



**Example trigger in this research context:** If you encounter "the Monster Group has order 8 × 10^53," search for: (a) the original Monster Group paper, (b) a mathematics monograph on finite groups, (c) a separate peer-reviewed reference that cites this order. Verify all three sources agree.

### Method: Contradiction Log

**What it is:** A dedicated running log of every contradiction, tension, or disagreement encountered between sources or within the framework's internal claims. Contradictions are not silently resolved by picking one side — they are documented and characterized.



**Why it is in this prompt:** Autonomous research agents tend to smooth over contradictions by picking the majority or most recent source, which hides real disagreement from the reader. The Kohärenz-Protokoll framework is complex and spans multiple domains; contradictions between them are precisely what an audit must expose.



**How to apply it — step by step:**



1.  Maintain a **dedicated Contradiction Log** section in your working notes and in the final output.



1.  For each contradiction you encounter:



  - **Axis affected:** Which of the 7 axes does this touch?
  - **Claim A:** First statement (with source)
  - **Claim B:** Contradicting statement (with source)
  - **Logical relation:** Are they truly contradictory, or merely in tension?
  - **Severity:** Is this a fatal logical crack, a minor inconsistency, or resolvable under reinterpretation?
  - **Resolution path:** Can this be resolved by (a) reinterpreting one claim, (b) finding a third source that clarifies, (c) accepting both as valid in different contexts?



1.  **Do not skip.** If sources disagree, log it. If internal claims within the framework seem to conflict, log it. The author can decide how to handle each contradiction; your job is to surface it.



1.  At the end, organize contradictions by severity: fatal cracks first, minor tensions last.



**When to stop / escape criterion:** You are done logging when you have reviewed all seven axes and their claimed justifications. Do not fabricate contradictions that do not exist, but do not overlook real tensions.



**Example trigger in this research context:** If Axis I claims that "AEGIS seeks coherence, which is K\_0," but Axis II claims that "multiplicity (K\_1) is more coherent under trauma," this is a tension worth logging and clarifying.

### Method: Adversarial Query Expansion (M13 — MANDATORY v2.1)

**What it is:** You start with a seed query vocabulary (the 7 axes and their stated theoretical grounding). Adversarial expansion means you deliberately generate **competing or orthogonal query vocabularies** to escape the risk that your seed queries miss important adjacent claims.



**Why it is in this prompt:** The Kohärenz-Protokoll framework is self-contained and coherent. This is both its strength and a risk: you might only search for sources that confirm the 7 stated axes and miss critiques, alternatives, or orthogonal framings that would challenge or enrich the framework.



**How to apply it — step by step:**



1.  **Seed vocabulary:** The 7 axes as stated: DKT, TSDP, Dramatica, Moonshine, Dialetheism, Reader Function, Climax Inversion.



1.  **Generate competing vocabularies:**



  - **Vocabulary A (Critique):** Search for "criticisms of Landauer's Principle," "limitations of TSDP in complex cases," "challenges to paraconsistent logic in narrative."
  - **Vocabulary B (Adjacency):** Search for "information loss in complex systems," "multiplicity in identity theory beyond TSDP," "observer effects in quantum narratives."
  - **Vocabulary C (Orthogonal):** Search for "thermodynamics of meaning," "entropy in communication theory," "Monster Group symmetries in other domains."



1.  **Expand at checkpoints:** After completing 2–3 axes, pause and ask: "What important sources have I not found because I only used the seed vocabulary? What neighboring fields should I check?"



1.  **Record expansions:** Document each new vocabulary you generate and what results it yields. If it produces no new relevant sources, note that too.



**When to stop / escape criterion:** Stop when three consecutive vocabulary expansions yield no new peer-reviewed sources relevant to the framework. You have likely reached the frontier of published work.



**Example trigger in this research context:** After verifying Landauer's Principle (Axis I), expand to "entropy in symbolic systems" or "information theory in philosophy." This adjacent literature might offer perspectives the framework's authors did not cite.
